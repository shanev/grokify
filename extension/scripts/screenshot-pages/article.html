The machines that taught us to think

A short walk from brass gears to stored programs.

By the Field Notes desk · 6 min read

Long before silicon, Charles Babbage sketched the Analytical Engine, a machine of gears and punched cards that was never finished. It was Ada Lovelace who saw what it could become, writing what many call the first program.

A century later, Alan Turing gave the idea its mathematics with the Turing machine, while Claude Shannon showed that switching circuits could carry out logic itself.

Then came the room-sized ENIAC, and Grace Hopper's insistence that people should write programs in something closer to English.
